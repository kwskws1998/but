We thank the reviewer for the suggestion. We added a comparison with OB1-reader, an
established cognitive model that explicitly represents asymmetric visuospatial attention
during reading.

We chose Llama 3-8B as it achieved the higher mean downstream accuracy (averaged over
six seeds) than Llama 3-8B-Instruct. Across these six independent checkpoints, the mean
parameters were sigmaleft=0.804 and sigmaright=3.471. This corresponds to a 4.32:1 ratio of
the rightward and leftward widths. Also, all cognitive model comparison metrics were
computed separately for each checkpoint and then averaged across the six checkpoints.

Using 100 simulated trajectories generated from Provo passages (Lopes Rego et al., 2024),
we mapped OB1’s letter-level Gaussian attention at each fixation to the relative T5-token
positions used by ET1’s redistribution module. We evaluated skew=3, matching the released
code, and skew=4, corresponding to OB1’s published 4:1 right-to-left asymmetry. This
parameterization is motivated by human perceptual-span estimates of approximately 14-15
letters to the right of fixation versus 3-4 letters to the left (Rayner 1986, 1998, as cited in
Snell et al., 2018, p. 973).

The original OB1 model limited visual input to a five-word window for computational
efficiency (Snell et al., 2018, p. 973). In the released Provo implementation, this window is
coded as n-1, n, n+1, n+2, n+3, where n is the fixated word. The accompanying repository
records that the fixated word was moved to the second position of the five-word stimulus “to
allow for longer skips (over two words).” Thus, this right-heavy support is a documented
property of the released Provo simulation implementation, rather than a consequence of our
redistribution procedure.

We compared three unit-TRT allocation conditions: no redistribution, the asymmetric
redistribution, and a paired symmetric control designed to remove directional asymmetry
while preserving the learned kernel’s overall spread. For each checkpoint, the symmetric
control used a single shared left/right scale chosen to match the asymmetric kernel’s
token-space standard deviation on the same fixation contexts. This construction used only
the learned kernel parameters and token-support geometry—not OB1 attention or any
comparison metric. We set the source TRT to one so that the analysis reflected only the
redistribution pattern, independent of ET1 predictions.

At each fixation, we mapped OB1’s letter-level Gaussian attention over its five-word window
onto ET1’s T5-token positions. We then evaluated and normalized all three conditions over
exactly those mapped tokens. Depending on tokenization, the resulting window contained
3–19 T5 tokens per fixation and covered pooled relative positions from -10 to +15. Because
the released OB1–Provo window includes more words to the right, a symmetric Gaussian
can still produce unequal pooled left or right mass after being restricted to these tokens.
Here, symmetric refers to equal leftward and rightward Gaussian scales, not necessarily
equal pooled mass.

Because OB1 measures attention width in letters whereas ET1 redistributes TRT across T5
tokens, we compared spatial spread only after converting OB1 attention to the same
T5-token coordinates. Spatial spread was defined as the standard deviation of the
normalized weights across relative T5-token positions, centered on each distribution’s own
mean. For each checkpoint, the symmetric control was matched exactly to the
corresponding asymmetric kernel on this measure. This preserved the overall spread while
allowing their left–right directionality to differ. No redistribution retained all weight at the
source token and therefore had zero spread.

Spearman correlation measures whether OB1 and each condition rank the relative token
positions similarly: a high value means that positions receiving relatively high or low weight
under OB1 tend to receive correspondingly high or low weight under the redistribution rule,
regardless of the exact amount of weight. Jensen–Shannon (JS) divergence compares the
complete normalized allocation shapes, including how much weight is assigned to each
position. A JS divergence of zero indicates identical distributions, and lower values indicate
closer correspondence with OB1.

Both Gaussian conditions were closer to OB1 than no redistribution on both metrics. This
shows that fixation-centered spatial spreading explains most of the shared structure. Under
skew=3, the paired symmetric condition was closer to OB1 (Spearman: 0.9352 vs. 0.8952;
JS: 0.1324 vs. 0.1403). Under the human-motivated skew=4 condition, however, the
asymmetric kernel was closer on both metrics (Spearman: 0.9098 vs. 0.8976; JS: 0.1457 vs.
0.1553).

The differences between the two conditions are expected to be modest because, for each
checkpoint, the paired symmetric control was constructed to match the learned asymmetric
kernel’s overall token-space spread. The symmetric control used the same Gaussian width
on both sides, whereas the learned kernel used separate leftward and rightward widths and
consequently allocated more weight to the right. Their comparison therefore isolates
left–right directionality while controlling for how broadly weight was redistributed. Crucially,
their relative ordering reversed when OB1’s asymmetry increased from the released skew=3
setting to the human-motivated skew=4 setting: the learned asymmetric kernel became
closer to OB1 on both Spearman correlation and JS divergence. Because the two candidate
kernels were matched in overall spread, this reversal supports the learned kernel’s
directional allocation rather than a difference in candidate kernel width. It advocates
directional consistency with OB1’s 4:1 (right-to-left) setting.
Looking separately at the weight assigned to positions left of, at the center of, and right of
fixation makes the skew=4 result easier to interpret. The symmetric and learned asymmetric
conditions assigned nearly identical weight to the center (26.7% and 26.9%, respectively),
but differed in how they distributed the remaining weight. Compared with the symmetric
control, the asymmetric kernel reduced leftward weight from 26.7% to 11.4%, moving it
closer to OB1’s 2.0%, while increasing rightward weight from 46.5% to 61.7%. The
asymmetric kernel thus captures OB1’s strong rightward imbalance more clearly than the
symmetric control, although it assigned more total weight to the right than OB1’s 43.2%. This
overallocation partly offsets its improved correspondence on the left, explaining why its
advantage in Spearman correlation and JS divergence under skew=4 is observed but
modest. Accordingly, this comparison evaluates the spatial allocation component of OB1
within its simulated fixation contexts.
